September is here. The alarms are set earlier, the backpacks are heavy, and the fall sports season is in full swing. For a lot of young adults and student-athletes, this time of year is marketed as an exciting fresh start. But if we’re being honest? For those navigating ADHD and anxiety, the sudden shift from the unstructured days of summer to the high-pressure demands of the fall can feel less like a fresh start and a lot more like a freefall.



When the old structures of summer vanish and the new foundations of the school year haven’t quite solidified yet, the brain’s threat-detection system can go into overdrive. But big changes shouldn’t feel like a freefall. Whether you're stepping onto a new campus, starting a new degree, or walking into a new locker room, let's talk about how to build the floor you’re standing on.



Here is how you can stop fighting the transition and start setting yourself up for a resilient fall season.



**1. Quiet the "Loud Roommate" in Your Head**

When schedules get chaotic, anxiety loves to make an appearance. I always tell my clients in West Chester: Anxiety is a loud roommate.



When you’re staring down a syllabus with six upcoming deadlines or feeling the pressure of an upcoming game, that roommate starts shouting worst-case scenarios. We might not be able to completely kick that roommate out today, but we can definitely turn the volume down.



**How to do it:**

Create immediate anchor points. Predictability is the absolute best antidote to transition-related anxiety. Establish 2 or 3 small, non-negotiable routines in your first few weeks. It could be listening to the same playlist on your drive to school, a designated 15-minute cooldown stretch after practice, or grabbing coffee from the same spot on Tuesdays. These small, predictable routines provide your nervous system with much-needed data points of safety, lowering the volume of anxiety.



**2. Stop Fighting Your Brain (and Start Partnering With It)**

In my practice, I see so many high-achieving young adults and athletes with ADHD who carry a deep sense of shame when they struggle with seasonal transitions. They look around and think, "Why is everyone else handling this so easily?"



Here is the truth: your brain is simply being asked to perform complex executive-functioning tasks without the external scaffolding it needs to succeed. Stop fighting your brain and start partnering with it.



How to do it:

Let’s turn the chaos of "too much" into a strategy for "just enough." The sheer volume of a new schedule can lead to paralysis. Instead of trying to "get your whole life organized," focus on micro-goals. Break your day down into tiny, manageable steps. Don’t worry about passing the entire semester; worry about packing your gym bag the night before. By setting up environmental cues (like leaving your cleats by the door or putting your medication next to your toothbrush), you create the scaffolding your brain needs to thrive.



**3. Remember: Mental Resilience Keeps You in the Game**

For my student-athletes out there, the physical demands of fall sports are exhausting, but the mental demands are what usually lead to burnout. Physical talent gets you to the game; mental resilience keeps you in it.



You cannot let your inner critic be your toughest opponent on the field. If you are missing passes, dropping times, or making mistakes at practice, notice how you speak to yourself afterward. Are you analyzing the play, or are you attacking your character? Resilience is built by separating what you did from who you are.



**Finding Your Center**

Whether you are a young adult trying to navigate the stressors of life transitions, a student-athlete trying to quiet your inner critic, or a parent trying to bring joy back to the dinner table amidst the back-to-school chaos—you don't have to navigate it alone.



A major life transition does not have to be a period of crisis. With the right tools, it can become a powerful opportunity for growth. Let's build those strategies that honor how your brain actually works.
